Beyond the Ratio: Why Your Keto Macros Are More Than Just Percentages

2026-06-07 · Macros · Tags: macros, ketokitchen, Beyond

When most people start keto, they latch onto a single number: the 70-25-5 ratio. Seventy percent fat, twenty-five percent protein, five percent carbs. It’s a tidy formula that promises ketosis, but as a chef who has spent years cooking for a wide range of keto eaters, I can tell you that this ratio is more of a starting point than a rule. The truth is, your macros are not just about hitting a percentage—they’re about feeding your body the right amounts of the right nutrients to support energy, satiety, and metabolic flexibility. In this article, we’re going to peel back the layers of macro tracking and explore why the quality, timing, and absolute grams of your fat, protein, and carbs matter far more than any pie chart.

The Fallacy of the 70-25-5 Rule

I’ve lost count of how many clients have come to me frustrated, saying, “I’m hitting my macros perfectly, but I’m not losing weight.” When we dig into their food logs, the problem is almost always the same: they’re treating the ratio as a rigid law rather than a flexible guideline. The 70-25-5 split is based on the idea that a high-fat, moderate-protein, low-carb diet will keep you in ketosis. But here’s the catch: percentages are relative. If you’re eating 1,200 calories a day, 70% fat is only 93 grams of fat—which might not be enough to keep you satiated or to fuel your brain. Conversely, if you’re eating 3,000 calories, 70% fat is 233 grams, which could be excessive and lead to weight gain.

The real key is to focus on absolute grams, not percentages. Your body doesn’t care about ratios; it cares about the actual amount of each macronutrient you consume. For most people, a better starting point is 20-30 grams of net carbs, 0.8-1.2 grams of protein per pound of lean body mass, and the rest of your calories from fat. This approach ensures you’re getting enough protein to preserve muscle, enough carbs to stay in ketosis, and enough fat to feel full and provide energy. So, the next time you see a keto infographic with a perfect pie chart, remember that it’s a simplification—not a prescription.

Protein: The Misunderstood Macro

Protein is arguably the most misunderstood macro in the keto world. Many people fear that too much protein will kick them out of ketosis via gluconeogenesis, the process where the liver converts amino acids into glucose. While it’s true that excess protein can be converted to glucose, this process is demand-driven, not supply-driven. In other words, your body only makes as much glucose as it needs, and eating extra protein doesn’t automatically spike your blood sugar. In fact, for most active individuals, protein is the most important macro for preserving lean mass, supporting recovery, and keeping hunger at bay.

As a chef, I always encourage my clients to prioritize protein at every meal. Think of it as the anchor of your plate. A good rule of thumb is to aim for 30-40 grams of protein per meal, depending on your size and activity level. This could be a 5-ounce chicken breast, a 6-ounce salmon fillet, or a hearty serving of grass-fed beef. Don’t be afraid to go over your “protein limit” if you’re feeling hungry or if you’re training hard. The benefits of adequate protein far outweigh the negligible risk of gluconeogenesis. And remember, protein also has a higher thermic effect than fat or carbs, meaning your body burns more calories digesting it—a win for your metabolism.

Fat: Not All Fats Are Created Equal

When we talk about fat on keto, we’re not just talking about quantity—we’re talking about quality. The source of your fat can dramatically affect your energy levels, inflammation, and even your ability to stay in ketosis. For example, a diet heavy in industrial seed oils like soybean or canola oil can promote inflammation and disrupt your metabolic health, even if you’re hitting your macro targets. On the other hand, fats from whole foods like avocados, olive oil, nuts, seeds, and fatty fish provide essential fatty acids and fat-soluble vitamins that support everything from brain function to hormone production.

In my kitchen, I use fat as a flavor vehicle, but I also choose it wisely. I cook with butter, ghee, and coconut oil for high-heat methods, and I drizzle extra-virgin olive oil over finished dishes for a dose of polyphenols. I also incorporate fatty fish like salmon and mackerel for their omega-3s, and I snack on macadamias and almonds for a satisfying crunch. The point is, don’t just count grams of fat—consider the quality. A tablespoon of grass-fed butter is not the same as a tablespoon of margarine. When you’re planning your meals, aim for a variety of fat sources, and avoid anything that’s highly processed or hydrogenated. Your body will thank you with better energy and clearer skin.

Carbs: The Quality and Fiber Factor

Carbs on keto are often treated as the enemy, but they’re not all bad. The key is to focus on net carbs—total carbs minus fiber and sugar alcohols—because fiber doesn’t raise blood sugar and can actually support gut health. Many people make the mistake of eating too many “keto-friendly” processed snacks that are loaded with sugar alcohols like maltitol, which can spike blood sugar and cause digestive distress. Instead, I recommend getting your carbs from nutrient-dense, low-glycemic vegetables like leafy greens, broccoli, cauliflower, zucchini, and bell peppers. These foods provide fiber, vitamins, and antioxidants that are essential for long-term health.

When you’re tracking your carbs, don’t just look at the number—look at the source. A cup of spinach has about 1 gram of net carbs, while a cup of kale has 2 grams. But a low-carb tortilla might have 6 grams of net carbs, yet it’s made from refined almond flour and contains additives. The quality of your carbs matters. I always tell my clients to “eat the rainbow” within their carb limit. That means choosing a variety of colorful vegetables to ensure you’re getting a broad spectrum of phytonutrients. And don’t forget about fiber—it’s a carb that works for you, not against you. Aim for at least 25-30 grams of fiber per day, which is higher than the typical keto intake, but it’s doable if you prioritize leafy greens, chia seeds, flaxseeds, and avocados.

Adjusting Macros for Your Unique Body

One of the biggest mistakes I see is people using a generic macro calculator and never adjusting based on their own results. Your macros are not set in stone—they should evolve as your body changes, as your activity level shifts, and as you move through different phases of your keto journey. For example, if you’re feeling sluggish and your workouts are suffering, you might need to increase your protein or even add a few more carbs (like sweet potato or berries) to fuel your performance. If you’re not losing weight, you might need to reduce your fat intake, especially if you’re eating too many fat bombs and not enough protein.

The best way to find your optimal macros is to start with a baseline, track your results for two weeks, and then make small adjustments. I recommend using a food scale and an app like Cronometer or MyFitnessPal to get accurate data. Pay attention to your energy, hunger, mood, and ketone levels (if you’re testing). If you’re in ketosis but feeling miserable, something is off—maybe you need more electrolytes, or maybe your fat intake is too high and your protein too low. The goal is to find a balance that keeps you in ketosis while also supporting your overall well-being. Remember, keto is not a one-size-fits-all diet; it’s a metabolic state that you can fine-tune to your unique physiology.

Conclusion

As a chef, I’ve learned that the best recipes are not about following a formula—they’re about understanding the ingredients and how they work together. The same goes for your macros. Instead of obsessing over the 70-25-5 ratio, focus on the absolute grams of protein, fat, and carbs that your body needs. Prioritize high-quality protein, choose fats from whole food sources, and get your carbs from nutrient-dense vegetables. And most importantly, listen to your body. Your macros are a tool, not a cage. By moving beyond the ratio and embracing the nuance, you’ll not only stay in ketosis but also feel your best, both in the kitchen and in life. So, go ahead—cook with intention, track with curiosity, and let your macros serve you, not the other way around.
